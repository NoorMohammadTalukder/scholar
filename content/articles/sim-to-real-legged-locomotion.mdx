## Introduction

Legged robots promise access to places wheels cannot reach, but training them in the real world is slow and dangerous. Simulation is fast and safe, and it is also wrong in ways that matter: contact dynamics, motor latency, and terrain compliance are all approximations.

## Approach

Our policy consumes proprioceptive history and the output of a terrain encoder trained to predict ground properties from recent foot contacts. In simulation we randomise friction, stiffness, and geometry across 40,000 procedurally generated terrains.

## Experiments

We deployed the policy on a 12 kg quadruped across six outdoor sites. Success was defined as reaching a waypoint 10 m away without a fall.

| Terrain | Trials | Success |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gravel | 30 | 100% |
| Construction rubble | 30 | 93% |
| Scree slope (25°) | 30 | 90% |
| Stairs (17 cm rise) | 30 | 97% |

## Discussion

The terrain encoder is the key contribution. Ablating it drops rubble success to 61%. We conjecture that explicit terrain estimation gives the policy a compact, transferable representation that raw proprioception lacks.

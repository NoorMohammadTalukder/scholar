## Introduction

Mobile money has spread across sub-Saharan Africa faster than any financial technology in history. Its welfare effects remain contested because adopters differ from non-adopters.

## Data

We link the Ghana Living Standards Survey panel to administrative adoption dates from two mobile network operators for 4,120 households observed between 2014 and 2024.

## Identification

We exploit staggered rollout of agent networks across districts, using a difference-in-differences estimator robust to heterogeneous treatment effects.

## Results

Adoption raised per-capita consumption by 6.1% (95% CI 3.4–8.8). Following a health or weather shock, adopting households were 11 points less likely to fall into poverty, an effect driven almost entirely by remittances from relatives in cities.

<Callout title="Heterogeneity">
Households without a migrant member saw consumption gains of only 1.8%, statistically indistinguishable from zero.
</Callout>

## Conclusion

Mobile money works as insurance, not as a savings technology. Policy that treats it as the latter will misallocate effort.

## Introduction

Histories of the metric system tend to be written from Paris outward. Adoption elsewhere appears as a series of dates. This article reverses the perspective and asks what adoption looked like from inside a state that was simultaneously trying to build railways, collect taxes, and convince European powers that it deserved treaty revision.

## Sources

I draw on three archives: the internal correspondence of the Ministry of Agriculture and Commerce, the records of the Tokyo Chamber of Commerce, and a set of 41 merchant ledgers from Osaka that record transactions in both *shaku* and metres for over a decade.

## A commodity-by-commodity settlement

The ledgers reveal that the shift to metric units happened at strikingly different rates for different goods. Textiles converted within three years; rice was still traded in *koku* well into the twentieth century.

> The state could decree a unit, but it could not decree a habit.

## Conclusion

Standards, I argue, are best understood as a measure of state capacity rather than of scientific modernity. The Meiji government's success with textiles and failure with rice maps precisely onto its ability to inspect and tax each trade.
